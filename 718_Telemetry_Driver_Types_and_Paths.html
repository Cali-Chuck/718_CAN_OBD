Choosing a Telemetry Path

The sheer number of telemetry signals combined with the variety of possible methods of obtaining them can make it hard to know where to start.

To clarify the valid choices and help with your decision making, here is an intentionally oversimplified set of four different telemetry "paths." You can use this to quickly down select the options that apply to your particular and unique combination of vehicle features, driving experience, comfort with vehicle modification and data objectives.

Obviously this framework won't apply to every driver, for instance there are plenty of "expert / racer" drivers who employ the Garmin catalyst device as their primary "coach" and would happily add an OBD connector to get a little extra data were Garmin to ever offer that capability. This chart also does not take into account the plethora of third party aftermarket sensors for things like real time tire temperature and pressure sensing.

Here are the key takeaways from this chart:

  • Drivers of every skill level can benefit from telemetry!
  • This is especially true for novice drivers who have access to data "coaches." Drivers can progress their skills very quickly with just 2-3 "core" data signals (accelerator & brake pedals plus speed).
  • The bulk of "core" driver telemetry can be obtained without any car modification by simply adding a Bluetooth OBD dongle that is compatible with the lap timer program you are using. There are multiple dongle options but a lot of us use the products from OBDLink the connect wirelessly to a phone using Bluetooth.
  • There are two different kinds of signals on the OBD port – OBD "Basic" or OBD-B and OBD "Extended" or OBD-E. The two types are explained later on in this document.
  • The only reason to access a CAN bus is to obtain "pro" level driver and engine telemetry. The main difference between "pro" and "core" is data frequency – OBD doesn't come very fast, and slows down quickly the more data you request. CAN is up to 50X faster than OBD with no ceiling on requests.
  • "Deep" engine Telemetry exists on 718 but is only of practical use to a very narrow set of enthusiasts who are actively modifying their engines, playing with fuel types and/or modifying air/fuel mixtures. Even most expert / racer drivers do not require the bulk of the vast telemetry that is available from a modern ECU.

With all this in mind, let us consider each of the four telemetry paths in turn, including the typical attributes of each type of driver and the specific data signals / channels of interest that are available to each one.

Driver #1 – Core lap timer telemetry

For the motorsport driver that just wants the basic car telemetry captured by a lap timer for their own analysis or working with a data coach. This category also applies to private coaches who temporarily install their own Race Navigator, VBox or Aim Solo type hardware in a student's car and wish to plug an OBD dongle in to get core telemetry.

All this driver needs is a basic OBDLink MX+ style device. An external GPS like a RaceBox or similar is strongly recommended given the inaccuracy of phone GPS and IMU at track speeds. A typical channel set for Driver #1 contains just five signals (gear will be specific to transmission type):

Driver #2 – Core Driver Data + Basic Engine Data

In addition to the core driving data above, Driver #2 wants to keep an eye on engine health using via a simple OBD connection. A key use adding a light set of engine metrics is to understand the "normal" baseline values for things like key temperatures and pressures to spot problems before they become failures or codes. Another common use for such data is to be able to work backwards to possible root cause after a car throws a code on the track to help diagnose/debug a problem.

With all 5 "core" plus 10 "basic engine" channels, you will quickly discover how fast OBD slows down. A typical update rate for these 15 total channels is no better than 1 once per second per channel. At this point the OBD port is exhausted – adding more channels will just degrade data quality. Pruning the list will visibly improve the update rate.

Driver #3 – Expert driver needing high-frequency driving data for coaching

Expert drivers / racers typically want the highest update rate possible in order to study the effects of their driving inputs on a millisecond-by-millisecond basis.

At this point CAN tapping becomes mandatory because of the OBD speed limit. The Drive CAN is strongly preferred, but the Chassis CAN is possible (at the cost of some channels not being available – these are identified in the interactive signal tables below) if the specific vehicle lacks an accessible Drive CAN. Here's a typical channel set for such a user. A high-precision external GPS is also mandatory in order to accurately geo-locate CAN telemetry at millisecond update rates.

CAN fully replaces OBD for this type of driver / telemetry path. Not only is CAN data up to 50X faster than OBD, but in general it is more accurate. Porsche "smooths" a number of data channels sent over the OBD port, as well as artificially truncates some data fields. CAN data is ultra-accurate because it's what the engine uses to make realtime decisions.

Driver #4 – Engine modder / tuner playing with fuel types, air flow, mixtures & timing, wheel slip and more

This driver is actively modifying the engine guts through custom intakes, turbos, valves, cylinders, custom cams, water/meth injection, fuel pumps, extra radiators, or whatever. S/he is also almost certainly running a pro tune and periodically sending data logs off somewhere for analysis & map adjustments.

This user wants everything that Driver #3 has, plus as much of a realtime X-ray of the engine as is possible to obtain.

You can see that a simultaneous combination of CAN and OBD modes is required to meet the needs of Driver #4. In particular because many of the "commanded" signals are not on CAN, and these are required to evaluate the "measured" signals that are. This requires two OBDLink type devices – one configured for OBD and one for CAN – which RaceChrono does support.

Remember the OBD speed limit still applies. It pays to choose the 10 or at most 12 signals that are most important to your use case. Do not attempt to capture every single one of these channels via OBD-B or OBD-E.

One of us (@Cali_Chuck) uses both CAN and OBD-E simultaneously with two OBDLink MX+ sending telemetry to RaceChrono. Because while CAN is far richer than Basic OBD, on 718s it does not have a number of very desirable fields for anyone doing engine tuning. For example, turbo boost (actual) is available over CAN but the commanded boost (set point) is only on OBD-E. It is very possible to run multiple OBDLink MX+ devices concurrently with a lap timer on an iOS or Android device.

Driver #4 with a Cobb ECU tune may have to choose between have a Cobb Datalogger on the OBD port, or putting an OBDLink device on it. The advantage of going with OBD-B / OBD-E is that the resulting telemetry is both (1) time synchronized with the rest of the data stream; and (2) it is properly geotagged for track location. The disadvantage is that Cobb has some data channels that do not exist – or at least no one has yet discovered – on OBD or CAN.

Despite what some people claim – it is NOT POSSIBLE to run two active devices simultaneously on the car's OBD port. OBD ports are engineered to have a single active device connected to them. Amazon does indeed sell "OBD splitters" but they are intended for the situation where the second OBD device is 100% "passive" and never transmits. Not only that, but ECUs themselves are not designed to handle overlapping OBD requests from multiple devices. In other words, an ECU working on a request from OBD device #1 ignores inbound requests from OBD device #2 because it is not currently listening. This behavior can be easily seen by study OBD packet traces.

Decision Tree for CAN Bus Access

The biggest factor in the decision to jump from OBD to CAN is deciding where and how to obtain access to a CAN bus. This decision is further complicated in turn by the multiplicity of vehicle configurations – principally 6MT vs. PDK and whether the Soundaktor is present or deleted. This diagram shows a basic decision tree to help aspiring CAN "tappers" to decide how to approach the challenge:

  • Generally tapping a single CAN bus is all that is required. Capturing multiple CANs is possible but outside the scope of this tutorial.
  • PDK owners should plan to access the Drive CAN because there are up to 4 different places around the vehicle to reach it that appeal to a range of wiring skill levels.
  • 6MT owners only have access to the Drive CAN from the CAN gateway under the dash. Unless they are comfortable modifying wiring in this area, the only alternative is to tap the Chassis CAN in the right rear of the vehicle where it meets the EPB controller.
  • Once you've chosen the access location, the final decision is how to tap in. There are two methods.
    • "Pin split & breakout" avoids any physical penetration of CAN wiring, so it can be less risky. However, this requires you to obtain a two-way or "Y" harness adapter with pre-installed connector pins. This harness is installed by disconnecting the existing CAN pins from the main wiring harness, and re-installing them into the "Y" harness, thus forking / splitting the signal into a breakout cable.
    • "Posi-tap" is far less complicated than the pin breakout method, but requires a special adapter that physically pierces the CAN wire to reach the copper inside. Though there are probably over a thousand Porsche owners with taps like this, some users have had bad outcomes that required dealer repairs.
